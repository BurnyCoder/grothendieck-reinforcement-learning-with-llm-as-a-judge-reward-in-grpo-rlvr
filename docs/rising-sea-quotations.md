## Linear algebra and vector analysis: Rising Sea

Source: *Linear Algebra and Vector Analysis*, Math 22B, Unit 15, “Rising sea,”
on [Oliver Knill's Harvard course site](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~knill/teaching/math22b2019/handouts/lecture15.pdf),
sections 15.1–15.3. This is the supplied version of the lecture commentary, not a
single direct quotation from Grothendieck. It differs from the linked handout:
the supplied parenthetical list of fields is absent there, and the handout adds
a sentence about determinants. The [course page](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~knill/teaching/math22b2019/)
states that the author placed the course documents in the public domain.

> Alexander Grothendieck was a special mathematician. Both extremely creative, charismatic as well as eccentric, he not only revolutionized huge parts of mathematics (modern algebraic geometry, commutative algebra, homological algebra, sheaf theory, category theory), he also chose in the later part of his life to shut himself off from the world and live alone as a hermit. Today we learn about a picture which Grothendieck drew about methods to solve mathematical problems. It is the hammer and chisel principle versus the rising sea approach to solve mathematical problems.
>
> The Hammer and Chisel principle is to put the cutting edge of the chisel against the shell and to strike hard. If needed, begin again at many different points until the shell cracks—and you are satisfied. Grothendieck puts this poetically by comparing the theorem to a nut to be opened. The mathematician uses the Hammer and Chisel to reach "the nourishing flesh protected by the shell". It can also be called the Sledgehammer method. You just hit the problem with all you have and grind it through until the problem is solved.
>
> The rising sea approach submerges the problem first into a theory, going well beyond the results originally to be established. Grothendieck imagined to immerse the nut in some softening liquid until the shell becomes flexible. After weeks and months, by mere hand pressure, the shell opens like a perfectly ripened avocado.

## nLab's description

Source: [“The Rising Sea,” nLab](https://ncatlab.org/nlab/show/The%2BRising%2BSea).
This introductory explanation is nLab commentary, not Grothendieck's own prose.

> The “rising sea” is a metaphor due to Alexander Grothendieck (see the quote below), meaning to illuminate how the development of general abstract theory eventually brings with it effortless solutions to concrete particular problems, much like a hard nut may be cracked not immediately by sheer punctual force, but eventually by gently immersing it into a whole body of water.

## Grothendieck's translated nut and rising-sea passage

The user supplied this passage via [nLab](https://ncatlab.org/nlab/show/The%2BRising%2BSea).
[Colin McLarty's *The Rising Sea: Grothendieck on simplicity and generality I*](https://ncatlab.org/nlab/files/McLartyRisingSea.pdf),
opening page, quotes it from *Récoltes et Semailles*, pages 552–553, and identifies
the translations in his paper as his own. The supplied punctuation is retained.

> I can illustrate the second approach with the same image of a nut to be opened.
>
> The first analogy that came to my mind is of immersing the nut in some softening liquid, and why not simply water? From time to time you rub so the liquid penetrates better, and otherwise you let time pass. The shell becomes more flexible through weeks and months – when the time is ripe, hand pressure is enough, the shell opens like a perfectly ripened avocado!
>
> A different image came to me a few weeks ago.
>
> The unknown thing to be known appeared to me as some stretch of earth or hard marl, resisting penetration… the sea advances insensibly in silence, nothing seems to happen, nothing moves, the water is so far off you hardly hear it.. yet it finally surrounds the resistant substance.

## Video commentary on theory building and AI mathematics

Source supplied by the user: [3Blue1Brown, “There's never been a better time to go into math”](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKrwhjOI8Nc).
The following is the user's supplied transcript excerpt. Its wording has not
been independently checked against the audio or assigned timestamps. These are
the video commentator's arguments, including the mountain metaphor and views on
proof and AI; **they are not quotations from Grothendieck**. The opening maxim's
original attribution is not established by this archive.

> One nice quote on this matter is that good mathematicians prove theorems, great mathematicians make conjectures, and the greatest mathematicians write definitions. And so we can even add that to our list. You know, aside from proving theorems, you have the exercise of deciding what's even worth asking. There's a lot of taste making in that. Having a certain sense for when an idea might be interesting enough that it produces good math.
>
> And then instead of saying write definitions, I'm gonna go ahead and throw in a third category of theory building. You know you have a bunch of theorems that are proved or maybe you have some sense of what questions are worth asking. But we actually wanna get out of it is like a broad understanding of of what's really going on here. You know, when Descartes introduced Cartesian coordinates, it wasn't so much any one question that was being answered, it's just an entire framework of thinking. Or when Galois was introducing the very seeds of group theory, the significance of that had very little to do with, you know, the unsolvability of the quintic. It's that it was a new way of conceptualizing what was going on that would produce an incredibly flowering portion of math that was highly useful for the centuries thereafter.
>
> I think Grothendieck is a good example of this too. He had all sorts of metaphorical ways of speaking but one of them was if you're trying to get from the top of one mountain to another you know one way is to solve all the problems to like go down the mountain and then go up the other and the other would be to have a rising sea that lifts up between the mountains and just sail from the top of one to the other and the analogy that rising sea is kind of all of the theory that you build not necessarily proving one particular theorem but giving a deeper overall understanding. understanding to what's really going on.
>
> And with all these categories, another implicit part of what's going on is clearly communicating what was done to others. It's not just vomiting out a proof, it's giving a reasonably motivated exposition of what that is. Or in building the theory, it's not just like listing a bunch of definitions, the great mathematicians will effectively communicate why these are the structures worth engaging with. And try to convey some of their taste in that.
>
> It's not the proof of the unsolvability of the quintic that matters, it's the creation of group theory, which in turn has effects like like having people postulate that quarks might exist. It's not the yes or no answer to Fermat's last theorem, it's all of the math that sprung forward from it and its applications.
>
> For example, what does the practice of doing math actually look like if the machines are better at the proof generation? Well, if we take that optimist's perspective, the one who's exhilarated by it, it basically looks like leaning into the theory building. You are no longer rate limited by how quickly you can solve certain problems along the way. You can ask all of the questions that you want. You can much more quickly see what the solutions to them are. You can try to parse that and just understand how does this fit into a bigger narrative, what are the elements of simplicity among all of the proofs that I'm looking at, and how can I weave this together into a fabric of a new kind of theory, where Galois was introducing group theory or Grothendieck was developing algebraic geometry. What is it that you as an individual get to entirely grow out? Maybe your PhD thesis is not a handful of a few proofs, it is that entire theory building.
>
> And for so much of the history of math, we have used proof as the proxy for understanding. It is the north star to shoot for where to prove something means that you understand it. We have these grand open problems and we consider the moment that a proof is created to be the moment they are solved.
>
> I think at this point we should consider proof to be kind of the same as numerical calculation, which is to say it's a form of raw data that is helpful for theory building, but it is certainly not the end in and of itself. This is not my own thought by the way, other people have framed the idea of these machine-generated proofs as being treated a little bit more like data. But I think very broadly, there is something liberating in thinking of theory building as being the goal, not proof, and proof as being a kind of raw data that you can plug into that.
>
> For example, I do not care about the forced blow-up to the Navier-Stokes problems. What I would care about is if somebody understands the ideas that went into that proof and the kind of mathematics that can flower out of those ideas into a beautiful coherent theory. And if someone puts in the work to doing that, Give them the Millennium Prize problem. That's the reason that we care it in the first place. The proof is just data, it's like giving me more digits of π.
>
> This also influences how you do research. Maybe sometimes what you need is one more proof and interacting with the model is the best way to do that. But often it's probably not and what's better is to close the laptop, scribble through your own understanding of it, and ask, what's blocking me right now from developing a deeper theory for what's going on? Is it one more proof? Or is it something about my understanding of the existing proofs?
>
> But what if, the grad student today might ask, the theory building side of it falls as well, and the most effective way to push forward theoretical math in some abstract sense is not just to have the machines generate the proofs or to ask what theorems they should prove, but they're also digesting all of those proofs, finding the elements of simplicity in it, understanding what truly novel fabrics you need to weave in order to come up with a coherent theory around it. It would be the analog of giving it all of math up to the... up to the 1500s and then having it not only solve cubic equations but come up with the right notation for how to even represent it come up with the theory of complex numbers you know all of the downstream things like that
>
> I think for the foreseeable future, research needs to place more and more of an emphasis on the theory building side of math, which it always has, that always has been the point. But the main difference is that proof was a very valuable proxy for that, and it's losing its value as a proxy. At this point it's a form of raw data, it stands to be a useful form of raw data, and yes, undoubtedly we have to change how we measure human academic contribution. But that's probably a good thing. Proof in and of itself has probably been overvalued for decades.

## Social-media commentary

Source supplied by the user: [@littmath's post](https://x.com/littmath/status/2109653733470572878).
The source could not be retrieved during this documentation check (HTTP 403), so
the supplied wording is preserved without claiming independent authentication.
Only the nested passage is attributed to Grothendieck; the surrounding
interpretation is the post author's commentary.

> In his memoir, “Récoltes et Semailles” (Reaping and Sowing), Grothendieck explains his approach to problem solving and mathematics more generally: "The unknown thing to be known appeared to me as some stretch of earth or hard marl, resisting penetration… the sea advances insensibly in silence, nothing seems to happen, nothing moves, the water is so far off you hardly hear it... yet it finally surrounds the resistant substance." He compared a precise mathematical question to a nut, which, rather than being cracked open by a chisel, yields at the perfect time to simple hand pressure once it has been soaked in the ocean of mathematical understanding. According to this account of mathematical progress, solutions, though important, are ancillary to the advance of the rising sea of mathematical understanding. At best they measure it to some extent, but the goal is to raise the water level.

## Artificial Grothendieck Intelligence (AGrI)

Source supplied by the user: [@aran_nayebi's post](https://x.com/aran_nayebi/status/2057592779308429687).
This is the user-supplied wording, attributed to that post rather than to
Grothendieck. Direct retrieval returned HTTP 403, so independent authentication
is not claimed. It proposes a future research direction; the present project does
not claim to have achieved AGrI, solved the named open problems, or demonstrated
the proposed research-agent organization.

> I think the next milestone will be "Artificial Grothendieck Intelligence" (AGrI): defining new general mathematical structures to solve the hardest of open problems as special cases, like the Riemann Hypothesis or P vs. NP.
>
> But for the "very big" problems, maybe extensive search through constructions in the vast existing literature is not enough. Maybe what is needed for those problems is closer to Grothendieck-style mathematics: inventing the right ambient language in which the original problem becomes a special case of a more general structure. That's what I mean by Artificial Grothendieck Intelligence (AGrI). Not merely AI that proves theorems, but AI that invents the new mathematical objects in which the theorems become *inevitable*.
>
> And why stop at one AGrI? You could imagine simulating something like the IHES school: manager agents dividing a research program into subprograms, subagents pursuing lemmas for hours or days, other agents distilling the resulting abstractions, checking them, and communicating the useful pieces back upward.
>
> One reason Grothendieck's IHES school was so successful is that its abstractions were relatively human-compressible. Once you adopted the relative perspective, the ideas could propagate through the community.
>
> But maybe that constraint has also been a bottleneck. Maybe many longstanding open problems, like those in number theory which Grothendieck felt was the hardest nut to crack, have solutions that are checkable in principle, but whose motivating abstractions are not human-compressible.
